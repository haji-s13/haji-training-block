# Nutrition for an Endurance Runner: Daily Training, Threshold Days and Race Day
**Prepared:** 2026-11-23 | **Research question:** What does current evidence recommend for daily macros, fueling around threshold and double-threshold sessions, race week and race day for a distance runner on about 80 to 180 km a week? | **Audience:** Self-coached distance runner (assumed)

> Not medical or dietetic advice. These are population ranges from position stands. A sports dietitian can tailor them, and anyone with signs of low energy availability (see Finding 7) should see a doctor.

## Executive Summary

**Question:** How much carbohydrate, protein and fat should a distance runner eat, and when, across training, threshold days and racing?

- **Carbohydrate should track the day's work, not stay fixed.** Position stands give 5 to 7 g/kg on moderate days (about an hour of training) and 6 to 10 g/kg on heavy days of 1 to 3 hours. A double-threshold day sits at the top of that range. (High confidence) [1][7]
- **Protein is 1.4 to 2.0 g/kg a day, spread out.** Aim for about 0.3 g/kg (20 to 40 g) every 3 to 4 hours, including straight after hard sessions. Fat fills the rest, usually 20 to 35% of energy. (High) [1][2]
- **Race fueling depends on duration.** For events over 90 minutes, carb load at 10 to 12 g/kg for 36 to 48 hours, eat 1 to 4 g/kg in the 1 to 4 hours before the start, then take 30 to 60 g/h in a half marathon and 60 to 90 g/h in a marathon. 5 km and 10 km races need a normal high-carb day before and little or nothing during. (High) [1][11]
- **Double-threshold days need fast refuelling between sessions.** With under 8 hours between sessions, take 1.0 to 1.2 g/kg/h of carbohydrate for the first 4 hours after the morning session. This is borrowed from general recovery guidance; no study has tested fueling for double-threshold days specifically. (Medium) [1][3]
- **Under-fuelling is the main risk at high volume.** The 2023 IOC consensus links low energy availability to bone stress injuries, hormone disruption and worse performance. (High) [5]

**Bottom line:** Eat more carbohydrate on and around threshold days and less on easy days, keep protein steady through the day, practise race fueling in training, and treat stalled performance, frequent injuries or lost periods as warning signs of under-fuelling.

**Overall confidence:** High for daily and race ranges; medium for the timing advice on double-threshold days.

## Background & Context

Glycogen (stored carbohydrate in muscle and liver) is the main fuel at threshold pace and above. Running at the second lactate threshold, the intensity the Norwegian method targets, burns mostly carbohydrate, so the amount stored before a session and the speed of refilling after it set how well the next session goes [1][7]. Sports nutrition guidance moved in the 2010s from a single high-carb diet to "periodized" fueling: eat more carbohydrate for hard or long sessions and less for easy ones [1][7].

All quantities here are per kilogram of body weight (g/kg). The tables use a **65 kg runner** as a worked example; scale to your own weight.

## Methodology

- **Searched (2026-11-23):** the 2016 ACSM/Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics/Dietitians of Canada joint position, ISSN position stands on protein, nutrient timing and caffeine, the 2007 ACSM fluid position stand, the 2023 IOC REDs consensus, and peer-reviewed trials and reviews from 2017 to 2025.
- **Older sources:** the core position stands date from 2007 to 2017. They are still the current official versions and are used because no newer versions exist; newer trials are added where they change the picture.
- **Not readable:** a 2025 *Sports Medicine* review (site error) and one full-text glucose-fructose trial (blocked). Neither is cited.
- **Assumptions:** healthy adult, races from 5 km to marathon, 80 to 180 km a week. Mapping weekly volume to the position stands' hours-per-day bands is my own interpretation.

## Detailed Findings

### Finding 1 — Daily macros should change with the day's training

The joint position stand gives these carbohydrate ranges [1]:

| Training load | Carbs (g/kg/day) | 65 kg runner |
|---|---|---|
| Light (low intensity or skill work) | 3 to 5 | 195 to 325 g |
| Moderate (~1 h/day) | 5 to 7 | 325 to 455 g |
| High (1 to 3 h/day, moderate to high intensity) | 6 to 10 | 390 to 650 g |
| Very high (over 4 to 5 h/day) | 8 to 12 | 520 to 780 g |

**How this maps to weekly volume (analysis, not from the sources):** about 80 km a week is roughly 6 to 7 hours, or an hour a day, which falls in the moderate band. 120 to 180 km a week, often run as doubles, is 1.5 to 2.5 hours a day and falls in the high band. Very few runners need the very-high band.

**Protein:** the joint stand gives 1.2 to 2.0 g/kg/day [1]; the ISSN stand gives 1.4 to 2.0 g/kg/day, in doses of 20 to 40 g (about 0.25 to 0.4 g/kg) every 3 to 4 hours [2]. For a 65 kg runner that is about 90 to 130 g a day in 4 to 5 meals. A 30 to 40 g dose of casein (such as Greek yogurt or cottage cheese) before sleep is supported for overnight recovery [3].

**Fat:** 20 to 35% of total energy, and not below 20% because that limits fat-soluble vitamins and essential fatty acids [1]. In practice fat is what's left after carbs and protein.

**Train-low and low-carb diets:** doing some easy sessions with low glycogen can boost training signals in the muscle, but performance improved in only about a third of studies [7]. A long-term low-carb, high-fat diet raised fat burning but worsened economy and erased the training gains of elite race walkers [8]. **Confidence: High** for ranges; **Medium** for train-low benefits.

### Finding 2 — Fueling around threshold and interval sessions

Threshold sessions burn mostly carbohydrate, so they are "high-fuel" sessions in the periodized model [7].

| When | What | 65 kg example |
|---|---|---|
| 3 to 4 h before | Meal with 1 to 4 g/kg carbs, moderate protein, low fat and fibre [1][3] | 1.5 to 2 g/kg: 100 to 130 g, e.g. oats, banana, honey, yogurt |
| 30 to 60 min before (if no meal) | Small, easy snack, ~0.5 to 1 g/kg carbs | Toast with jam, or a gel and water |
| During | Under 45 to 75 min: water is enough; a carb drink or mouth rinse is optional [1] | For long threshold sessions (over 75 min), 30 to 60 g/h |
| Within ~1 h after | Carbs plus ~0.3 g/kg protein [1][2] | ~60 to 80 g carbs + 20 to 25 g protein |

**Early-morning sessions:** if a full meal isn't possible, a small carb snack (a banana, a gel or a sports drink) 15 to 30 minutes before a threshold session is a practical compromise. Fasted easy runs are acceptable; fasted threshold or interval work goes against the "fuel for the work" principle [7]. **Confidence: High** for ranges, **Medium** for the early-morning compromise (practical inference).

### Finding 3 — Double-threshold days need fast refuelling in between

Bakken's model puts two threshold sessions 6 to 8 hours apart. No study has tested nutrition for double-threshold days. The best available guidance is for any two sessions less than 8 hours apart: take **1.0 to 1.2 g/kg/h of carbohydrate for the first 4 hours**, in frequent small feeds, with higher-glycaemic carbs [1][3]. Adding about 0.2 to 0.4 g/kg/h of protein helps when carbs fall below that rate [3]. For a 65 kg runner, that is about 65 to 80 g of carbs an hour, or roughly 260 to 310 g across the 4 hours.

That is a lot of food. In practice runners can get there with a big breakfast plus a sports drink, followed by a carb-rich lunch and a snack. Lower-fibre choices (white rice, bread, juice, bagels) are easier to eat in volume and digest before the second session. Daily carbs on these days should sit near the top of the high band, about 8 to 10 g/kg [1].

**Confidence: Medium.** The refuelling rate is well supported; applying it to double-threshold days is an inference.

### Finding 4 — Race week: carb loading for long races only

- **Events under 90 minutes (5 km, 10 km, fast half marathons):** 7 to 12 g/kg in the 24 hours before is enough. A full load isn't needed because glycogen stores aren't the limiting factor [1].
- **Events over 90 minutes (most half marathons, all marathons):** 10 to 12 g/kg/day for 36 to 48 hours before the race. Modern loading doesn't need a depletion phase; a taper plus high-carb eating is enough [1][11].
- **Practical tips (analysis):** cut fibre and fat to make room for carbs and avoid gut issues; expect 1 to 2 kg of weight gain from stored glycogen and water, which is normal. Practise the loading menu before a key long run.

**Confidence: High.**

### Finding 5 — Race morning

- **Meal:** 1 to 4 g/kg of carbohydrate 1 to 4 hours before the start; more food further out, less closer in [1]. A common marathon choice is 2 to 3 g/kg about 3 hours out (130 to 195 g for 65 kg).
- **Fluid:** about 5 to 7 ml/kg at least 4 hours before, and another 3 to 5 ml/kg about 2 hours before if urine is still dark or scant [4].
- **Caffeine:** 3 to 6 mg/kg about 60 minutes before improves endurance performance; doses as low as 2 mg/kg may help, and above 9 mg/kg side effects rise [12]. Test it in training.

**Confidence: High.**

### Finding 6 — Fueling during the race

| Race duration | Carbs per hour | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Under 45 min (5 km, most 10 km) | None needed | [1] |
| 45 to 75 min | Small amounts or a carb mouth rinse | [1] |
| 1 to 2.5 h (half marathon, fast marathon) | 30 to 60 g/h | Single or mixed sugars [1] |
| Over 2.5 to 3 h (most marathons) | Up to 90 g/h | Needs glucose plus fructose ("multiple transportable carbohydrates") [1] |

**Newer evidence for higher intakes:** in a 2025 study of 8 elite marathoners, 120 g/h burned more ingested carbohydrate and cut the oxygen cost of running by 2.6% compared with 60 g/h. GI symptoms (nausea, fullness, cramps) were worst at 120 g/h, and the authors say gut training should come before trying it in races [6]. **Confidence: Low** for the benefit of going above 90 g/h in non-elite runners; one small study.

**Gut training:** two weeks of repeated carb intake during exercise reduced GI symptoms and carb malabsorption in runners [9]. A 2022 study in highly trained athletes found only minor effects [10]. **Practical:** rehearse race fueling in long runs and marathon-pace sessions, building up by about 10 to 20 g/h at a time (analysis). **Confidence: Medium.**

### Finding 7 — Recovery

- **Same day, another session coming:** 1.0 to 1.2 g/kg/h carbs for 4 hours (Finding 3) [1][3].
- **Next-day session:** total daily carbs matter more than exact timing; hitting the daily target restores glycogen within 24 hours [1][3].
- **Protein:** ~0.3 g/kg (20 to 40 g) soon after hard sessions and every 3 to 4 hours afterwards [1][2].
- **Fluid:** replace about 125 to 150% of the weight lost over the following hours, with sodium to help retain it [1][4].

**Confidence: High.**

### Finding 8 — Hydration and electrolytes

- **Goal:** lose no more than about 2% of body weight during exercise. Losses beyond that hurt endurance performance, especially in heat [1][4].
- **Don't overdrink:** weight gain during a race signals overdrinking, which risks hyponatraemia (low blood sodium), a dangerous condition [4].
- **Individual sweat rates vary widely,** about 0.4 to 1.8 L/h, and sweat sodium averages about 35 mmol/L with a wide range [4]. **Sweat test:** weigh yourself nude before and after a 60-minute run, add fluid drunk; each kg lost is about 1 L of sweat.
- **Sports drinks:** ACSM suggests about 20 to 30 mmol/L of sodium (roughly 460 to 690 mg/L) and 5 to 10% carbohydrate [4]. Heavy or salty sweaters (white residue on clothes) may need more sodium in long or hot races.

**Confidence: High.**

### Finding 9 — Low energy availability and REDs

Energy availability is the energy left for the body's basic functions after training. The 2023 IOC consensus defines Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (REDs) as the harm from low energy availability that is problematic (long-lasting or severe) [5]. Effects include:

- **Health:** bone stress injuries, low bone density, lost or irregular periods, low testosterone, poor immunity, low mood [5].
- **Performance:** reduced training response, lower endurance, worse recovery and concentration [5].

The consensus dropped the old idea of a single cutoff of 30 kcal/kg fat-free mass a day as a diagnosis, since effects vary between people [5]. Reported prevalence ranges from about 15% to 80% depending on the sport and population [5].

**Why it matters here:** a big jump in volume, such as moving from singles to Bakken-style doubles, can raise energy use by several hundred kcal a day. If intake doesn't rise to match, energy availability drops even without dieting (analysis). **Warning signs:** stalled or falling performance, frequent illness, a stress fracture, lost periods, low libido, constant fatigue. These warrant a medical check. **Confidence: High.**

## Analysis

- **The evidence is strongest where it's oldest and most general.** Daily ranges, carb loading and in-race rates are backed by decades of trials and repeated in every major position stand [1][3][11]. The newest findings (above 90 g/h, gut training) come from small studies, often in elite men [6][9][10].
- **Fueling vs. train-low is a real debate.** Researchers who favour periodization argue that some low-glycogen training adds adaptation [7]; others note that performance gains are inconsistent and that chronic low-carb eating harms economy [7][8]. A sensible middle ground is to keep easy runs on normal eating and fuel threshold and race-specific work fully.
- **Double threshold is a gap.** The refuelling guidance for sessions under 8 hours apart fits well, but no one has measured glycogen across a Norwegian-style day.
- **Most sources rely on male athletes.** Female athletes face higher REDs risk, and the IOC stresses screening [5].

## Recommendations

1. **Set three carb "gears" (High priority, low effort).** Easy days at about 5 to 6 g/kg; threshold or long-run days at 7 to 8 g/kg; double-threshold days at 8 to 10 g/kg.
2. **Keep protein steady (High, low).** About 1.6 g/kg a day as a midpoint, in 4 to 5 servings of 20 to 40 g, including one after each hard session and one before bed.
3. **Never do threshold work fasted (High, low).** At minimum, have a 30 to 60 g carb snack before.
4. **On double-threshold days, refuel hard after the AM session (High, medium effort).** Aim for 65 to 80 g/h of carbs for 4 hours (for 65 kg), using low-fibre foods and drinks.
5. **Rehearse race fueling (High, medium effort).** Practise gels and drinks during long runs, building toward 60 to 90 g/h if you're racing a marathon.
6. **Do a sweat test (Medium, low effort).** Use it to plan fluid and sodium for summer and race day.
7. **Watch for REDs signs when raising volume (High, low effort).** Track weight trend, energy, injuries and, for women, the menstrual cycle. Increase food when mileage goes up.

### Sample days for a 65 kg runner

**Double-threshold day (~22 km, AM 07:00, PM 16:30). Target ~550 to 650 g carbs (this plan totals about 600 g) and ~130 g protein (2 g/kg, the top of the range for a heavy day).**

| Time | Meal | Carbs | Protein |
|---|---|---|---|
| 06:15 | Banana + toast with honey, coffee, water | 50 g | 5 g |
| 07:00 | AM threshold (e.g. 5 × 2 km), water | - | - |
| 08:30 | Big breakfast: oats with milk, berries, honey; juice | 120 g | 25 g |
| 10:30 | Bagel with jam + sports drink | 80 g | 10 g |
| 12:30 | Lunch: white rice, chicken or tofu, low-fibre veg | 120 g | 30 g |
| 15:00 | Snack: rice cakes, honey, sports drink | 50 g | 3 g |
| 16:30 | PM threshold (e.g. 10 × 1 km), water or drink | 0 to 30 g | - |
| 18:00 | Recovery drink: chocolate milk | 50 g | 15 g |
| 19:30 | Dinner: pasta, lean protein, veg, olive oil | 120 g | 30 g |
| 21:30 | Greek yogurt | 10 g | 20 g |

**Easy day (~12 km).** About 5 to 6 g/kg (325 to 390 g carbs), normal meals with whole grains, fruit and vegetables; same protein pattern.

**Marathon race week.**

| Day | Plan |
|---|---|
| Race −3 to −4 | Normal high-carb training diet (7 to 8 g/kg) |
| Race −2 and −1 | Load at 10 to 12 g/kg (650 to 780 g); low fibre and fat; carb drinks, rice, pasta, bread, juice |
| Race morning (3 h before) | 2 to 3 g/kg (130 to 195 g) + 300 to 450 ml fluid; caffeine 3 mg/kg (about 200 mg) ~60 min before if practised |
| Race | 60 to 90 g/h from gels and drink (e.g. a 25 to 30 g gel every 20 min plus sips), with fluid to thirst and sweat-test plan |

## Limitations & Open Questions

- **Old position stands:** the ACSM fluid (2007) and joint nutrition (2016) stands are still current but predate newer work on very high carb intakes.
- **No double-threshold nutrition studies;** timing advice for those days is inferred.
- **High-carb (120 g/h) evidence** comes from one study of 8 elite men [6].
- **Unverified:** the 2025 *Sports Medicine* review on high-carb fueling could not be read.
- **Next to research if useful:** iron and vitamin D for runners (common deficiencies), sex-specific fueling, and caffeine strategies across a double-threshold day.
